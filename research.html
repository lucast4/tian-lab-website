How does the brain rapidly generate structured novelty?

We propose that the brain solves novel problems through two complementary operations: abstraction, which identifies reusable concepts and rules from experience, and recombination, which assembles those abstract elements into new configurations for unfamiliar challenges. We study these mechanisms through three complementary lines of work.

How do we recombine learned actions into novel ones?

We use cognitive-motor tasks to test how humans and animals recombine learned movement concepts according to generative rules. In drawing tasks, for example, subjects must produce novel stroke sequences assembled from previously learned action units — letting us watch recombination unfold in real time as behavior.

What kinds of neural networks can abstract and recombine?

We develop neural network architectures that explore how cognitive operations like abstraction and recombination can emerge from neural dynamics — connecting behavior to mechanisms that could plausibly be implemented by populations of neurons.

Where and how are these computations implemented in the brain?

Using large-scale electrophysiology and perturbation techniques, we map where and how abstraction and recombination are computed in frontal and parietal brain regions, linking circuit-level activity to the behaviors we observe.

Creative intelligence and cognitive disorders

The lab aims to identify the fundamental computations underlying creative intelligence — including planning, reasoning, and imagination — and to understand how disruption of these processes contributes to cognitive disorders.
